03 / Local retail

A shop with a beautiful shelf and a checkout that lost half its carts.

A neighborhood general store selling pantry goods, tools, and gifts, with an online store bolted on during a slow season and never revisited. Identity, packaging, and a storefront rebuilt around one fact: most customers are standing in the aisle holding their phone.

Role

Identity, packaging, e-commerce UX, front-end build

Scope

Brand system, 40-SKU catalog, storefront, label templates

Timeline

9 weeks, concept engagement

Stack

HTML/CSS/JS, Supabase, Stripe Checkout, Netlify

0 views

01 — Context

The situation

Fieldnote does the hard part well: buying, merchandising, and a physical shop people linger in. The online store was a template with 40 products, stock photography that did not match the shelf, and a five-step checkout requiring account creation.

Cart abandonment sat near 70%, and the shop's own staff routed customers away from the site to a phone order — the clearest possible verdict on the checkout.

02 — Problem

What was actually broken

  • Identity split in two — The in-store experience and the online one looked like different companies, so the site never inherited the shop's goodwill.
  • Checkout required an account — A five-step flow with an account wall on step two, for an average order of $34.
  • Photography mismatch — Supplier stock images meant the product on screen was not the product on the shelf.
  • No mobile consideration — The template was designed desktop-first in a business where 82% of sessions were phones.

03 — Approach

How the work was shaped

The identity work came first because the checkout problem was partly a trust problem. If the site does not look like the store, a card form is a bigger ask.

  • One identity across shelf and screen — A wordmark, a stamp mark, and a label system that works printed on kraft paper and rendered at 44px on a phone.
  • Guest checkout in two steps — Email, shipping, pay. Account creation is offered after the order is placed, when it costs the customer nothing to decline.
  • Product pages that answer aisle questions — Origin, size, what it goes with, and whether it is in stock at the shop right now — the four things staff get asked in person.
  • Photography direction, not photography — A shooting guide the owner can follow with a phone: same surface, same light, same angle. Consistency beats production value for a 40-SKU catalog.

04 — Decisions

Three calls worth defending

  • Kept Stripe Checkout instead of building a custom one — A hosted, familiar payment page converts better than anything a studio can hand-build for a shop this size, and it removes card data from the business entirely.
  • Put in-store availability on the product page — Half of online browsing was pre-visit research. Telling people it is on the shelf drives a trip, which is the higher-margin sale.
  • Designed labels before the site — The label constrains the palette and type in the least forgiving medium. Once it worked at 2×3 inches on kraft, the screen was easy.

05 — System

Color and type

Archivo at heavy weights for shelf-readable labels, mono for lot numbers and origin lines — the details customers photograph and send to friends.

The engagement produced a brand system that survives contact with a printer, a storefront that treats the phone as the primary device, and a checkout the staff no longer route customers away from.

Reflection

The temptation on a retail project is to spend the budget on the homepage. Nearly all of the value here sat in two screens — the product page and the payment step — and in a label template the owner can fill in themselves for the next hundred products.
